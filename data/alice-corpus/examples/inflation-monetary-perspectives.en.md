# Money creation, inflation and economic readings

## Questions covered

- Why do some people speak of inflation as soon as the quantity of money increases?
- Who benefits from money creation?
- Does creating money necessarily produce a proportional rise in prices?

## Short answer

A definition does not demonstrate a cause. Monetary growth, prices and redistributive effects must be distinguished. Austrian critiques notably examine the channels of credit creation and the recipients of new money; these mechanisms do not give an identical result in every context.

## Explanation

In the vocabulary of some Austrian authors, "inflation" refers to monetary expansion. Per Bylund nonetheless points out that Mises's definition includes the demand for money and that usage varies. This is not the same as price inflation measured by an index.

A commercial bank generally creates a deposit when it grants a loan. Capital, liquidity, funding, regulation and the demand for credit limit this creation; banks do not simply lend out a fixed stock of pre-existing deposits.

The analogy of a cake cut into more slices illustrates additional units against an unchanged quantity of goods. It becomes misleading if production, spending or the demand for money change. It does not make it possible to compute an inflation rate automatically.

Cantillon describes a diffusion through specific channels that changes spending and relative prices. The Austrian critique emphasizes the potential advantage of the first recipients and the distortion of signals. Studies of asset purchases also examine these effects, with results that depend on wealth, employment and the indicators chosen.

## Limits and confusions

Saying that redistributive effects exist does not prove that every policy always enriches the same people or increases every inequality indicator. The cited 2018 British study estimates a limited effect on the usual relative measures; it does not refute every distributional effect and does not automatically extend to other countries.

Political critiques of discretionary issuance can be presented and argued. They must be identified as analyses, without presenting a central bank's stated objectives as proof that there are no costs.
